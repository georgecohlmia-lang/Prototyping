STEP 01

Write the claim. Pick how real the test has to be.

Four levels. Go only as far up as your claim requires, because each costs more than the last.

From the lessons: the claim Upstream wrote in MVP Scoping, its level-three test of the longevity program, the four parts, the enrollment numbers, and the hours and scheduling from the cost record. The lessons don’t say which answer Upstream chose for the bottleneck, so that’s left for you. They also don’t say how many hours the trainer or the diet review took, so the log holds only the physician hours the lesson gives.
The claim · one sentence
Your claim is about delivery, so level three is the floor. Levels one and two can’t teach you anything about delivering the work.
Has level three stopped surprising you? If not, you’re paying for systems before you know what they need to do.

The parts you’ll deliver, one per line.

STEP 03

Keep two records. What they do, and what it costs you.

Record one · what the customer does

People don’t complain. They stop.

Record two · what it costs you

The record everybody skips. Log your own hours per customer, visible work and preparation separately. Then sort each line: does it get more expensive with each customer, or doesn’t it?

The bottleneck

The input nobody costed. Name it, and name the answer you’re choosing. What you can’t do is call it a scheduling problem and try harder.

STEP 04

Show the arithmetic. Out loud.

Multiply the first pile by every customer you’d eventually have. Eight people happily paying tells you nothing about whether you can serve eight hundred.

Usually not “it works” or “it doesn’t.” It’s what works at this shape, to this many people, with these people.

The trap · the demo that becomes a pitch

Someone hesitates, you handle the objection, and now you’re measuring your close rate. The tell is your own behavior.
